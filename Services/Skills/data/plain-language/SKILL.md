# Plain Language

Turns dense or specialist text into clear, direct prose that a general reader can understand on the first pass — without losing accuracy or important detail. Use when asked to "simplify this," "explain it simply," "remove the jargon," or make technical, legal, or academic content readable. The goal is clarity, not dumbing down.

## Rules
- One idea per sentence, and keep sentences short — aim for under 20 words.
- Use everyday words instead of jargon: "use" for "utilize," "help" for "facilitate," "start" for "commence."
- If a technical term is unavoidable, define it in plain words the first time it appears.
- Put the main point first; lead with what it means or why it matters before the details.
- Prefer active voice and real subjects: "We reviewed the data" not "The data was reviewed."
- Keep all numbers, conditions, and qualifications — plain language must stay precise.
- Use concrete examples and short analogies where they clarify an abstract idea.

## Before and after
Before: "Utilize the authentication mechanism to facilitate secure access to the platform."
After: "Sign in to access the platform securely."

Before: "In the event that payment is not remitted within the stipulated timeframe, service provision will be suspended."
After: "If you don't pay on time, we'll pause your service."

## Gotchas
- Do not delete important qualifiers just to make a sentence shorter; precision still matters.
- Do not rewrite domain terms that the audience needs — define them instead.
- Avoid turning a careful statement into an overconfident one.
- Do not sound condescending; write for a smart reader who lacks the background.

## Output format
- A simplified version in short, active sentences, with any unavoidable technical terms defined inline, followed by an optional one-line list of the terms you defined.
